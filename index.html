Exercises
Exercise 1 — A polygon made of regular polygons
Set d = 5 and leave every ak = 1, so that z is the regular pentagon. Look at the coefficient chart: only c1 is nonzero. Now drag z4 inward to a4 = 1/2.
  1. How many eigenpolygons appear? Compare their coefficients |cj| for j ≠ 1. What do you notice about their sizes?
  2. Tick Show basis polygons instead of eigenpolygons. Which Fj are convex, which are stars, and which are degenerate? How are F2 and F3 related?
  3. Open Numeric details and find the vertex reconstruction check. What does it confirm?
  4. Change d to 6, move one vertex, and look at the basis polygons again. Which ones are traced more than once, and how many times?
Show hint
Lowering one vertex by δ changes every coefficient by the same amount in modulus, because cj = (1/d) Σk ak ωk(1−j) and the term for k = 4 changes by δ ω4(1−j)/d. Here δ = 1/2 and d = 5, so c0, c2, c3 and c4 all have modulus 0.1, while c1 drops from 1 to 0.9. A local change to the shape is a global change to the spectrum.

F2 and F3 are the same pentagram traced in opposite directions, because ω3 is the complex conjugate of ω2. For d = 6, F2 and F4 trace a triangle twice, and F3 is a segment traced three times there and back. In general Fj is the regular star polygon {d/j}, traced gcd(j, d) times.

The reconstruction check confirms that adding the eigenpolygons vertex by vertex gives back z exactly, up to rounding: every polygon is a sum of regular, star or degenerate polygons.
Exercise 2 — Areas add up
Set d = 4, nspd = 1, na = 2 and granularity = 4, and drag the vertices to a = (1, 1/2, 1, 1/2). The polygon is a rhombus.
  1. Compute the area of the rhombus by hand from its diagonals, and check it against A(z) in Numeric details.
  2. Read the table of signed areas A(Ej). Which eigenpolygons contribute? Why is A(E3) negative? Check each value against A(Ej) = (d/2) |cj|2 sin(2πj/d).
  3. Check that Σj A(Ej) = A(z). Then move the vertices to several other configurations, including d = 5, and check the reconstruction error each time.
  4. Area is quadratic in the vertices: the area of a sum of two polygons is not, in general, the sum of their areas. Why does the sum work here?
Show hint
For the rhombus, c1 = 0.75 and c3 = 0.25, so A(E1) = 2 · 0.752 = 1.125 and A(E3) = −2 · 0.252 = −0.125. Their sum is 1, the area of a rhombus with diagonals 2 and 1. E3 runs clockwise, so its signed area is negative. E0 is a point and E2 is a segment, so neither encloses area.

The signed area is A(z) = ½ Im Σk zk zk+1. Expanding z = Σj Ej produces cross terms between Ej and El, and each of them contains the factor Σk ω(l−j)k, which is zero whenever j ≠ l. Only the diagonal terms survive. This is an orthogonality relation, of the same kind as Parseval's identity, and it needs the sum over all vertices to work.
Exercise 3 — Cell areas do not add up
Set d = 5, nspd = 1, na = 3 and granularity = 4, and drag the vertices to a = (1, 1/2, 3/4, 1/4, 1). Keep the signed measure. Click Export tables and open the spreadsheet.
  1. Add the coverage matrices A1, …, A4 cell by cell. (E0 is a point, so its table is omitted and its contribution is zero.) Compare the result with the coverage matrix A of z. Look in particular at the cell in sector 4, annulus 1.
  2. Compare the row sums, the totals per annulus, of A and of Σj Aj. Then compare the column sums, the totals per sector.
  3. Compare the grand totals. Which of the three comparisons succeeds, and why only that one?
  4. The column sums of A are all different, whereas the column sums of Σj Aj are all equal. Compute ½ sin 72° · as as+1 for each sector s. What do the column sums of A measure?
Show hint
In the cell at sector 4, annulus 1, z has measure 0.2083, while the eigenpolygons together give only 0.0252. The row sums of A are (0.7368, 0.2910, 0.0718) against (0.9735, 0.1261, 0) for the sum. The column sums of A are (0.2378, 0.1783, 0.0892, 0.1189, 0.4755) against 0.2199 for every sector of the sum. Only the grand totals agree, at 1.0997, which is Exercise 2 again.

The reason is that a cell measure integrates the winding number, and the winding number of z around a point is not the sum of the winding numbers of the Ej. In the rhombus of Exercise 2, for example, z winds once around the origin, while E1 winds +1 and E3 winds −1, so the eigenpolygons together wind zero times around it. The decomposition moves area from one place to another, and the gains and losses only balance over the whole plane.

With nspd = 1, the column sum for sector s is the area of the triangle (0, zs, zs+1), which is ½ sin(2π/d) as as+1. So the sectors of A record the products of adjacent values in the row of X, information that the eigenpolygons spread evenly across all sectors. In the rhombus of Exercise 2 the sector totals happen to agree, but only because of its symmetry; the annulus totals still disagree there.
Exercise 4 — Other senses of measure, and rank
Keep the configuration of Exercise 3.
  1. Switch to the unsigned measure and repeat the grand-total comparison. Does even the total survive now? Try the total-variation measure as well.
  2. Back in the signed measure, look at the coverage matrices A1, …, A4. What do you notice about their columns? Read off rank(Aj) below each table.
  3. What is the rank of Σj Aj? Compare it with rank(A), shown below the coverage matrix of z.
  4. What does this suggest about where the rank of a feature matrix produced by a polygon template comes from?
Show hint
In the unsigned measure the grand totals are 1.0997 for z and 1.2699 for the sum of the eigenpolygons. Signed measure lets the clockwise eigenpolygons cancel part of the counter-clockwise ones; the unsigned and total-variation measures drop orientation, so that cancellation is lost and nothing balances, not even in total.

Since d = 5 is prime, each Ej with j ≠ 0 looks the same after a rotation by 72°: the rotation only shifts which vertex comes first. With nspd = 1 that rotation maps each sector onto the next, so all columns of Aj are identical and rank(Aj) = 1. Their sum therefore also has identical columns and rank 1. Yet rank(A) = 3.

The coverage matrix of z owes its rank to the way the eigenpolygons interact on the lattice, not to the eigenpolygons taken one at a time. A spectral explanation of the features in terms of eigenpolygons alone would miss exactly the structure that makes a linear system SW = Y solvable.
